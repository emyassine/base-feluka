# Visual direction

A reusable, brand-agnostic art direction and production skill for websites and editorial products. Apply it in any repository or framework. Always inspect the active project's content model, visual identity, asset conventions, licenses and component architecture. For Numerimondes projects, also read [`README_ART_NUMERIMONDES.md`](README_ART_NUMERIMONDES.md).

This skill is written for coding agents. Follow the workflow in order. Do not shortcut it, and do not ship a visual just to finish the task. **A dull image is a failed image.** If you cannot reach the quality bar in section 2B, stop and report with concept options (section 11) instead of publishing something safe.

---

## 1. Brand essentials

Before visual work, locate the active brand guide, official logo, image palette, typography, and any owner-provided photography, drawings or art direction. Treat those source files and written brand rules as authoritative. Never redraw, distort or ask a model to invent a logo. Use approved fonts only when the project defines them; otherwise do not invent a typography system for raster artwork.

Read owner-provided visual references as authored work. Preserve their intent and ask before making a material transformation. Use signature materials or motifs only where the active brand guide calls for them; do not apply one visual motif to every page. Understand the *meaning* of the brand's symbols (for example what a logo represents) and let that meaning quietly inform the art direction even when the logo itself never appears in the image.

---

## 2. Core principle: one idea, one image, one reader

Every visual answers: **What is this page about, in one glance, for the person who will read it?** It must also give the reader a reason to look longer.

Preferred sources, chosen by subject fit, ambition and rights:

1. A real, permitted visual source: the owner's own photograph or drawing, an artwork, object, place or photograph with known provenance and usable rights.
2. A real source with a deliberate, authored, rights-compliant intervention (collage, surreal detail, light, scale shift, motion) that turns it into an idea. See section 2C for what is and is not allowed.
3. An original photographic still life, macro, rendered scene, illustration, engraving-style or print-style piece conceived for the page.
4. A procedurally generated or coded visual (shader, generative geometry, simulated light) when it expresses the subject.
5. A wholly AI-generated scene only when no better route exists and it can meet the quality bar.
6. A short motion-design loop (section 8) when motion adds meaning.

AI is a production tool, not the default source and not a substitute for an idea. Do not present a composite or generated image as documentary evidence, or fabricate events, client evidence, results, testimonials or identifiable people.

## 2A. Audience, stance and tone

Decide who the image speaks for: the organization, a client or partner, or a neutral explainer. Follow the active brand guide and the page's actual claims. State what the reader should understand and feel in one second. The feeling and visual register should suit the subject, audience and brand.

Defaults unless the brand guide says otherwise:
- Pages that set rules, terms, security, data handling or standards: the image speaks **for the organization in a position of competence and care**: precision, order, stewardship, control of the process.
- Pages about capabilities, projects and outcomes: the image shows **capability, momentum and the client in control**.
- Practical guides: a **neutral explainer** that clarifies a mechanism.

For sensitive subjects, keep the image respectful and accurate. Do not portray people as vulnerable, distressed, indebted or anxious unless the subject specifically requires it and the depiction is handled responsibly. Avoid sensational imagery, stereotypes, misleading security cues, or visuals that imply facts the page does not establish.

**First-glance test (mandatory).** Describe the finished image in one plain sentence as a stranger with no context would. What would they guess the page is about, and how would they feel? If the guess is wrong or ambiguous, or the impression conflicts with the page or brand, reject it.

**Interest test (mandatory, equal weight).** Would a design-literate person pause on this image, or scroll past it? Would it look at home in a good editorial magazine, a respected research publisher or a design-awards gallery? If the honest answer is "it looks like a stock photo", reject it, even if it passed the first-glance test.

Literal keyword matching is not subject fit. Translate what the page actually says into an image that clarifies its meaning; do not illustrate a keyword with an object or person.

## 2B. Creative ambition bar (mandatory)

The standard is **art direction, not illustration of keywords**. The reference level is leading editorial and research publishers: striking photography with a point of view, refined material and light studies, surprising but precise metaphors, and polished motion.

### Concept exploration before production

Never go from page to image in one step. Before producing anything:

1. Write the page's **claim in one sentence** (what it promises or asserts).
2. Write **three distinct concepts**, each one line, each built on a different idea and ideally a different technique (for example a real artwork with an intervention, a photographic light study, a coded or physical-simulation motion piece). Do not write three variations of the same object.
3. For each concept, name the **metaphor** (what it stands for), the **technique** and the **motion potential**.
4. Run each concept through the **cliché check** and the **ambition tests** below. Discard failures.
5. Choose one. Record the three concepts and why you chose it in the provenance record.

### Cliché check (reject unless transformed into something unexpected)

Do not use these as the subject, even when they appear to match the topic: padlocks, keys, shields, keyholes, card readers, badge scanners, doors, turnstiles, cloud icons, servers in rows, circuit boards, binary or matrix rain, hooded hackers, glowing globes, handshakes, brains, robots, floating dashboards, generic office meetings, laptops on desks, stock "team around a table", and people pointing at screens. A cliché can appear only as a minor element inside a surprising composition, never as the whole idea.

Also reject: flat grey concrete or empty corridors used as "security", generic corporate blue, sterile white studios, symmetrical AI "hero" renders, and anything that looks like a stock-site search result for the page's keyword.

### Ambition tests (all must pass)

- **Originality:** If you searched a stock site for the page's main keyword, would your image look like those results? If yes, reject.
- **Point of view:** Does the image make an observation about the subject, rather than merely labelling it?
- **Craft:** Is there a deliberate decision about light, material, scale, perspective and negative space that you can state in one sentence?
- **Memorability:** Could the reader describe it to someone else tomorrow?
- **Economy:** One idea, few elements, strong focal point.

### Techniques to draw from (not exhaustive)

- Real or public-domain art combined with a precise, surprising modern or scientific element (see 2C).
- Light as the protagonist: refraction, caustics, diffraction, interference, shadow play, prisms, crystals, glass, water, aperture slits.
- Macro and material studies: stone, metal, ceramic, fibre, paper, textile, ink, oxidation, frost.
- Scientific and technical imagery treated as art: instruments, diagrams-as-objects, specimens, optical experiments, engraving or herbarium aesthetics.
- Print and craft languages: risograph, cyanotype, lithograph, woodcut, embroidery, zellige and other regional craft where relevant.
- Sculptural still life with a few forms and one accent.
- Coded or simulated visuals: shaders, particle or field simulations, generative geometry, 2.5D parallax from layered artwork.
- Photography with a point of view: unusual vantage, long exposure, shallow depth, dramatic natural light, real places with character.

## 2C. Using real art creatively and responsibly

Real art is a strong starting point, but recognizable works must be treated with respect and intent.

Allowed: deliberate, authored interventions on **public-domain** works or works with explicit adaptation rights, when the intervention is the idea. Examples of the kind of move: a surreal added object, a scale shift, a collage with a modern or scientific element, an animated light change, a layered parallax. The result must be clearly a transformation, credited as such, and must serve the page's claim.

Not allowed:
- Using AI to extend, outpaint, re-light or "fix" a recognizable work only to make it fit a crop. If it does not fit, choose another crop or another source.
- Altering religious or sacred imagery, or any work whose license forbids derivatives.
- Imitating a living artist's distinctive style, or modifying a living artist's work without permission.
- Choosing an artwork because it is famous, or because it matches a keyword literally.
- Presenting a transformed artwork as the original.

Always record the exact work, creator, institution, license, reproduction source, every change made and why.

## 3. Subject-fit rule (mandatory before any research or generation)

Before choosing or creating a visual:

1. Read the **entire page**: frontmatter (`title`, `summary`, `description`, `tags`, `category`, status, any image/video metadata) and the full body. Note the topic, audience, specific claims and tone.
2. Write the page's claim, the three concepts and the **fit sentence** (section 2B): "This image fits this page because ___." The sentence must refer to the page's actual content, not to the fame, beauty or novelty of the image.
3. If the sentence is weak, vague or generic, do **not** generate. Research a better source or clarify the concept with the owner.

Hard rules:
- Never copy a metaphor from an example, another site or another card onto an unrelated page.
- Never reuse one picture for unrelated topics.
- A visual can be beautiful and still be wrong for the page.
- Metaphors are derived from the page's claim, not from a keyword table. If you catch yourself reaching for the first object associated with the topic, discard it.

Sensitive subjects (privacy, security, legal, recruitment, personal data): the image should feel calm, precise and authoritative, and it may be abstract, symbolic, scientific or artistic. No fear imagery, no hackers, no identifiable private individuals, no depiction implying that the image shows the organization's real infrastructure unless it does.

## 4. Rights, provenance and honesty

Before using any real source:

- Confirm the license or permission for the exact reproduction. Prefer public-domain or open-access collections from museums and libraries, openly licensed archives, the project owner's own photography, or commissioned work. For anything else, check the exact license terms.
- Preserve required attribution. Never strip credit that the license requires.
- Record provenance for every asset in a sidecar note or in the project's existing metadata system: source URL, creator, date, license, any modification (crop, composite, AI-assisted edit), tool used, and the date it was added. Provenance must also state the **stance**, the **page claim**, the **three concepts considered and the reason for the choice**, the **fit sentence**, and the **first-glance and interest test results**. A provenance entry that only lists the source is incomplete.
- Never copy, hotlink or re-use another publisher's images, videos, layout assets or brand elements as the active site's content. Other sites can inspire general approaches, never supply unlicensed assets.
- Do not claim a production method that you cannot verify. Describe images as "composite", "AI-assisted", "photograph" or "rendered" only if you know it.
- Respect cultural and religious heritage (including Moroccan, Amazigh, Arab and Mediterranean craft and art). Credit it, do not caricature it, do not use sacred objects as decoration.
- If rights are unclear, **stop and ask**. Do not use the image.

### Realism, originality and attribution (mandatory)

- When a page benefits from documentary photography, search for a real photograph before generating an image. Inspect the actual image at full size; search-result descriptions are not enough. Verify that its subject, context, crop and rights fit this page, and that it passes the interest test. A technically free stock photo of a literal object does not pass.
- If generation is the right choice, aim for work with real photographic or material plausibility: believable optics, exposure, lighting, perspective, materials, imperfections and context. Avoid the polished, weightless, symmetrical look of generic AI renders. Do not claim that generated work is indistinguishable from photography; inspect it critically and reject visible synthetic artifacts.
- Never imitate a living artist's or photographer's distinctive style, recreate a copyrighted composition, or use another publisher's image as a production input without permission. References may guide general qualities such as light or composition, never reproduce protected expression. Do not remove watermarks or credits.
- Record every third-party artwork or photograph in the active site's attribution registry and show required credits in the place the brand guide defines. Keep a source link, creator, title, license, changes and local asset path.
- Attribution requirements still apply when a license does not legally require a credit; record provenance consistently. For CC BY-SA or similar share-alike sources, check what the adaptation requires before publishing.

## 5. Visual language and quality bar

Overall feel: editorial, idea-led, poetic, precise, tactile, with a clear point of view. One strong focal subject, simple composition, deliberate light, real texture, generous negative space.

Do:
- One metaphor per image. The subject must read clearly at thumbnail size (about 160 px).
- Use a small number of materials and forms.
- Keep a coherent but not identical palette across a collection. A set may share lighting and calm tonality; individual images vary in medium, technique and color. A collection that looks like twelve variations of one render is a failure.
- Use the active brand palette when it helps; let a real source's natural colors remain when documentary fidelity matters.
- Leave breathing room where text overlays will sit.

Do not:
- Bake text, logos, watermarks, numbers, UI mock-ups or signatures into generated images, in any language, including decorative pseudo-writing. Add any text later as a separate real layer using an approved project font.
- Produce six-fingered hands, melted faces, impossible objects, garbled lettering or plastic-looking surfaces. Inspect at 100 percent zoom before accepting an image.
- Fill the frame with clutter, neon glow, generic "AI gradient" swirls, floating dashboards, robots, brains, circuit boards or glowing globes.
- Use a signature material or motif by reflex.
- Show real brands, trademarks or copyrighted characters.

## 6. Formats by use

Always read the active project's components to confirm actual crops before producing assets. Defaults when nothing else is defined:

| Use | Aspect ratio | Master size | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collection card cover (articles, policies, legal notices, capabilities, projects) | Component-defined | 2048 px or higher | Follow the actual card crop and leave overlay space only when the component places text on the image. Optimize responsive renditions for display size. |
| Page hero / banner | 16:9 or 21:9 | 3200 px wide | Keep focal subject within the central safe area so it survives responsive crops. |
| Page or section background | Wide, tileable or soft | As needed | Low contrast, no focal clutter, must stay readable under text in both themes. May be a quiet motion field (section 8). |
| Open Graph / social share | 1.91:1 (1200 x 630 px delivered; produce master at 2400 x 1260) | | Keep important content inside the central 80 percent. Render title text as a real layer using an approved font. Add the real logo as a separate layer when appropriate. Must be legible at 600 x 315 px. |
| Square social | 1:1 | 1080 x 1080 or higher | Same rules as OG. |
| Poster | Project-defined | | Confirm before producing. |

For OG images specifically:
- Compose them from a real layout (HTML/SVG/Canvas template or the project's OG generator) so text is crisp and uses the approved font. Do not ask an image model to render the title.
- Background art may be the page's card visual, a material field allowed by the brand guide, or an image tied to the page.
- Check contrast between text and background (WCAG AA or better for large text) and test with the longest title in the collection.
- Provide a version that works on dark and light social surfaces; avoid pure white edges that vanish on light feeds.

Keep the source or master at the highest practical quality. Create optimized web renditions for the actual display sizes. Never upscale a small image and call it a master.

## 7. Production workflow

1. **Discover the project.** Find the content model (frontmatter fields for image, alt, video, poster), the asset directories, the card and hero components, the build or image pipeline, the OG generator if any, approved fonts and logo. Note existing visuals in the same collection so the new one belongs to the set without duplicating it, and note which techniques the set already uses so the new one adds variety.
2. **Read the full page.** Frontmatter and body (section 3).
3. **Write the claim, the three concepts and the fit sentence** (sections 2B and 3). If everything is weak, stop.
4. **Choose the concept, source and treatment.** Confirm rights (section 4).
5. **Decide the motion plan** (section 8). Plan the still first; plan motion when it adds meaning.
6. **Produce one asset at a time.** Review each result before starting the next. Never batch-generate a large set and pick afterwards. When using AI, give it a permitted real reference whenever one exists, state the subject, materials, light, camera and mood, and exclude text, logos and watermarks. Prefer the owner's own drawings and photographs when provided and relevant.
7. **Review at real size** (section 10). Reject and redo rather than patch a weak image. Do not lower the bar to finish.
8. **Move the selected files** from any generator output folder into the project's established asset location with descriptive, stable filenames. Never leave a site-referenced asset only in a tool's private output folder. Do not overwrite an existing asset unless explicitly asked.
9. **Integrate** using the project's own conventions: frontmatter fields, component props, accurate alt text. Confirm every referenced path exists.
10. **Preview** the still card, the hover state (if any), the mobile and reduced-motion fallback, light and dark themes, and the OG preview, at the actual crop.
11. **Report** the final file paths, source and license, the claim, the concepts considered, the fit sentence, what was verified and anything left undone.

Descriptive filename pattern (adapt to project convention): `{collection}-{page-slug}-{short-concept}-{variant}.{ext}`, lowercase, hyphenated, no spaces, no version suffixes like "final2".

For tool-specific prompts, local paths, palette, languages and brand-specific art direction, read the companion guide for the active brand. Never assume this skill's own example paths or one brand's look apply elsewhere.

## 8. Motion design

Motion is a first-class tool of the visual language, not a rare exception. The still image is always the resting state and the fallback; motion extends the idea.

**Where motion applies:** card hover or focus loops, hero and section backgrounds, OG animated variants where supported, and short feature loops. Aim for motion on a meaningful share of a collection's cards (for featured, flagship or conceptually rich pages first), but only when the movement carries the idea. Never add motion that is mere decoration.

**What good motion looks like:** one restrained, intentional action with real physics or craft: light sweeping across a faceted surface, a beam passing through an aperture only when aligned, ink or particles resolving into form, a slow camera move through layered artwork, a parallax built from separated planes, a material slowly catching light, a simulation settling. Smooth easing, seamless loop, no jitter, no sudden flashes.

**How to produce it (choose the lightest tool that reaches the bar):**
- Layered 2.5D parallax or light-sweep built from the still's separated layers (ffmpeg, Python with numpy and Pillow, or a canvas capture).
- Procedural or shader motion rendered headlessly (for example a WebGL or canvas scene captured with Playwright, then encoded with ffmpeg).
- 3D scenes rendered from Blender in the sandbox when light, glass or material behavior is the idea.
- Frame-by-frame or code-driven motion (SVG, Canvas, p5, Three.js, Remotion) when it suits the concept.
- AI video only as an assistive source for a real or authored reference, inspected frame by frame for artifacts and loop quality; never as an unreviewed shortcut.

**Technical rules for video files:**
- Length: about 5 to 6 seconds, seamless loop, **no audio track**, one restrained action.
- Behavior on cards: still image at rest. On pointer hover **and** on keyboard focus, reveal and play the video over the same image area. Pause and reset when the interaction ends.
- Attributes: `muted`, `playsinline`, `loop`, `preload="none"` (or equivalent lazy behavior), and the cover still as `poster`.
- Do not download any card video when the page opens. Load or start it only upon hover or focus.
- Touch devices, `prefers-reduced-motion: reduce`, playback failure or missing file: show the still only. Nothing should break or flash.
- Encoding: compact H.264 MP4 (add WebM only if the project already supports it), sized for the displayed crop, with a crisp poster that is a frame of the same artwork. Balance file size, sharpness and start time on representative desktop and mobile connections. If the video makes the page heavy or slow, reduce it or drop it.
- Keep the video file separate from the editorial image, and always provide the poster.

**Code-driven motion (no video file)** is allowed for backgrounds and heroes when it stays light, respects `prefers-reduced-motion`, pauses when off-screen, and never blocks content or hurts Core Web Vitals.

Shared card components:
- Support video as an **optional capability**. Keep the default image-only API and behavior unchanged; accept optional `video` and `poster` inputs.
- Do not hardcode any site's asset directory, frontmatter field names or URLs into a reusable component. Connect them through the owning site's content and asset conventions.
- Ensure accessible behavior: the card stays keyboard-operable, video is hidden from assistive technology (it duplicates the still), and no information is conveyed only by motion.

## 9. Performance, formats and accessibility

Performance:
- Use responsive sources (`srcset`/`sizes` or the framework's image component) where supported.
- Use modern formats (AVIF or WebP with a fallback if the project requires) without visibly sacrificing detail.
- Lazy-load below-the-fold images. Do not lazy-load the main above-the-fold image (the likely LCP element); give it explicit width and height to avoid layout shift.
- Typical targets: card renditions as light as possible while sharp at their display size; OG images under roughly 300 KB when possible, and well within platform limits; card videos compact enough to start quickly on mobile connections.

Accessibility:
- Write alt text that describes the **meaningful visual content** in one clear sentence, without repeating the card title and without keyword stuffing. Describe the visible subject and its meaning accurately; do not infer details the image does not show.
- If an image is purely decorative and the component supports it, use an empty `alt=""`.
- Check text legibility on every overlay (gradient scrims, contrast ratio) in both light and dark themes.
- Never convey meaning through color alone.

## 10. Review checklist (every asset, before calling it done)

- [ ] Full page (frontmatter and body) was read; claim written; three concepts recorded; fit sentence still true.
- [ ] Stance chosen and target feeling stated; no vulnerability, bills, debt, anxiety or period-drama cues unless the subject requires it.
- [ ] Cliché check passed: the subject is not a banned cliché object, and the image would not blend into stock-site results for the keyword.
- [ ] First-glance test passed: a stranger's one-sentence description matches the page topic and the target feeling.
- [ ] Interest test passed: a design-literate viewer would pause on it; it belongs in a good editorial context.
- [ ] Craft decisions about light, material, scale, perspective and negative space can be stated in one sentence.
- [ ] No AI extension, outpainting or fix-up of a recognizable work; any authored transformation is deliberate, credited and allowed by its license.
- [ ] Source, license and attribution confirmed and recorded.
- [ ] No baked-in text, logo, watermark or garbled glyphs; the real logo was never redrawn.
- [ ] No anatomical, physical or material artifacts at 100 percent zoom.
- [ ] Readable at thumbnail size; subject centered with room for overlays where the component needs them.
- [ ] Not a duplicate or near-duplicate of another card; the set shows variety of technique, not twelve variations of one render.
- [ ] Correct aspect ratio and crop for the actual component; safe areas respected.
- [ ] Renditions generated; file sizes reasonable; format supported by the project.
- [ ] File lives in the project's asset location; the path in content/frontmatter resolves; no files left only in a generator's output folder.
- [ ] Alt text is accurate and does not repeat the title.
- [ ] Contrast and legibility pass in light and dark themes.
- [ ] Motion (if any): meaningful, seamless 5 to 6 second loop, muted, not preloaded, poster present, hover and focus work, reduced-motion and touch show the still, failure falls back gracefully, frames inspected for artifacts.
- [ ] OG image (if any): text rendered as a real layer in the project-approved typeface, legible at 600 x 315, central safe area respected, tested with the longest title.
- [ ] Final report lists paths, provenance and anything that needs the owner's decision.

## 11. When to stop and ask the owner

Stop and ask rather than guess when:
- The page's subject is ambiguous or the fit sentence is weak.
- None of the three concepts passes the cliché, first-glance and interest tests. **Do not ship a safe image to finish the task.** Report the three concepts, what blocked each, and ask the owner to choose or supply material.
- Rights or provenance of a source are unclear.
- The project-approved font or official logo file cannot be located.
- The request would require showing real people, real client names, real results or real incidents.
- A change would overwrite or replace an existing approved visual.
- The component does not support a needed capability (video, srcset) and adding it would change a shared API.
- A page is a draft, and it is unclear whether its visual should signal draft or ready status.
- The stance is not obvious from the page.
- The first-glance or interest test fails twice in a row for the same page.
- The needed motion technique is not available in the environment (for example no way to render 3D or capture a canvas).

When asking, batch **all** open questions into a single message with a recommended default for each, so the owner can answer once.

---

## 12. Reporting format

At the end of each task, report briefly:

1. Page(s) covered, the claim and the fit sentence for each.
2. The three concepts considered and why one was chosen.
3. Source and license (or "generated from scratch" / "AI-assisted from reference X"), and the technique.
4. Final file paths (still, video, poster, OG) and where they are referenced.
5. Checks performed from section 10 and any that could not be performed.
6. Open questions or follow-ups.

Do not claim an image is integrated until the referenced file exists and the crop has been inspected.
